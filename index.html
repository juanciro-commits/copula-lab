Start here · why go beyond correlation

Correlation and its regression line

The Pearson correlation and its regression line are the most basic tools for describing how two variables move together. The correlation measures the linear association; the regression line uses it to predict Y from X, with slope b₁ = r · sy / sx. They work very well when the relationship is linear, as with normal variables. But they only see the linear part.

Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions

Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.

Y is always exactly \(e^{X}\). The relationship is perfect. Will the Pearson correlation be 1?

No. The points lie on a curve, not a line, so Pearson stays below 1. Kendall's \(\tau\) is exactly 1, because a larger X always comes with a larger Y.

Is the regression line of Y on X the same as the line of X on Y?

No. One minimizes vertical distances and the other horizontal ones. They only coincide when the correlation is perfect. The correlation itself is symmetric.

Can a few extreme points change the correlation a lot?

Yes. Pearson uses the actual values, so a handful of far-away points can pull it up or down. Rank-based measures like Kendall's \(\tau\) barely move.

Where it works

The relationship is a straight line with even scatter, for example two jointly normal variables like fathers' and sons' heights. Then the correlation describes the dependence completely, and \(r^2\) is the share of the variance of Y that the line explains.

Where it struggles

A curved relationship from skewed variables; heavy tails or outliers that pull the line; dependence that lives in the extremes. With different marginals, Pearson may not even reach 1 when the relationship is perfect.

What we need instead

A way to measure dependence that ignores the shape of each variable: ranks, Kendall's \(\tau\) and, finally, copulas. The rest of this page builds that toolkit step by step.

Review A · before we start

Density, probability and notation

A quick refresher on the three ideas every later step relies on: a pdf gives heights, probabilities are areas, and the cdf accumulates those areas. Pick a distribution and drag the two ends of the interval.

Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions

Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.

A pdf has height 3 at some point. Is that an error?

No. The height of a pdf is a density, not a probability, so it can be larger than 1. Only the total area has to equal 1.

For a continuous variable, what is the probability of getting exactly 5?

Zero. A single point has no width, so it has no area. Probabilities come from intervals.

What is the difference between \(f(y)\) and \(F(y)\)?

\(f(y)\) is the height of the density at \(y\). \(F(y)=P(Y\le y)\) is the accumulated probability up to \(y\).

How do you get \(P(a\le Y\le b)\) from the cdf?

\(F(b)-F(a)\): the accumulated probability up to b minus the accumulated probability up to a. It equals the area under the pdf between a and b.

A Uniform(0, 0.25) has height 4. Why must it be exactly 4?

Its width is 0.25 and the total area must be 1, so the height is \(1/0.25=4\).

What do the uppercase \(Y\) and the lowercase \(y\) mean in \(P(Y\le y)\)?

\(Y\) is the random variable, the number that has not come out yet. \(y\) is a specific value it could take.

Review B · before we start

Joint, marginal, conditional and independence

The same ideas behind every multivariate model, first with a table you can count. An ice-cream shop records 100 days by weather and sales. Move the slider to change how much the weather affects sales, and pick a weather row to see its conditional distribution.

Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions

Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.

If you know it is a hot day, does that change your guess about the shop's sales?

Yes, if the two are related. That updated guess is a conditional probability: sales given the weather.

How would you get the share of hot days from a table of weather by sales?

Add up the whole hot row. Summing over the other variable gives the marginal.

If weather and sales were unrelated, what would the chance of a hot day with high sales be?

The chance of a hot day times the chance of high sales. Multiplying the marginals is what independence means.

Moving the dependence slider changes the inside of the table. What never changes?

The row and column totals, the marginals. The slider only changes how the two variables pair up, which is exactly the job of a copula.

How do you check independence for one cell?

Compare the cell with the product of its row total and column total. If every cell equals that product, the variables are independent.

What is the continuous version of “conditional times marginal”?

\(f_{XY}(x,y)=f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x)\): the model from step 2.

Step 1 · Probability integral transform

Transformations: any distribution into a uniform

Start here: every copula is built on this tool. Pass a variable through its own cdf, U = F(Y), and the result is always Uniform(0, 1). The distribution of Y sits on top, sharing the horizontal y axis; the cdf in the middle is drawn the usual way, with y across and u = F(y) up; and the uniform sits on the right, sharing the vertical u axis. Each color holds 20% of the probability on both sides.

Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions

Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.

Take any list of exam scores and replace each score with its percentile. What shape would a histogram of the percentiles have?

Flat. Every 10% slice of percentiles holds exactly 10% of the students, whatever the shape of the original scores. That flat shape is a Uniform(0, 1).

Can a probability be larger than 1? Can a density?

A probability, never. A density, yes: a Uniform(0, 0.25) has height 4, because only the area has to equal 1.

Most exponential values sit near 0. To give every 20% of the probability the same amount of space, which part of the axis must be stretched?

The crowded part near 0 gets stretched and the long right tail gets squeezed.

After Check your understanding · 3 questions

Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.

What function turns any continuous Y into a Uniform(0, 1)?

Its own cdf: \(U=F(Y)\).

For an exponential with \(\theta=1\), what is \(u\) when \(y=1\)?

\(u=1-e^{-1}\approx 0.63\): the value 1 is at the 63rd percentile.

Why does the cdf climb steeply exactly where the pdf is tall?

Because the slope of the cdf is the pdf: \(F'(y)=f(y)\). Where a lot of probability sits, the accumulated probability grows fast.

Step 2 · Two variables at once

Level sets: a lognormal X and a normal Y given X

Before separating marginals from dependence, see how two variables combine into one joint density. This is the model from the board: first choose how X behaves on its own, then how Y behaves once X is known. Multiplying the two layers gives the joint density, fXY(x, y) = fY|X(y | x) · fX(x), and its level sets are the rings where that density is constant.

Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions

Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.

On a topographic map, what does each contour line connect?

Points at the same height. Here the height is the joint density, so each ring connects pairs \((x,y)\) that are equally likely.

If you know a father's height, does it change your best guess for his son's height?

Yes. That updated guess is a conditional distribution, \(f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\).

Income cannot be negative and has a long right tail. Is a normal a good model for it?

No. A lognormal fits better: always positive and skewed to the right.

Business example

Daily demand and daily revenue

X = daily demand, in hundreds of units. You know it well from historical data, and it follows a lognormal: always positive, usually moderate, with occasional very busy days.

Y = daily revenue, in thousands of dollars. You know how revenue behaves once demand is known: at $15 per unit, 100 units bring about $1,500, so revenue sits around μ(x) = 1.5·x, give or take about $300 from discounts and returns (σ = 0.3).

With those two layers you never model revenue directly, yet its whole distribution follows from them: the curve on the right of the plot. That lets you answer questions such as how likely a day with more than $3,000 in revenue is.

This is deducing, not estimating: no data is fitted here. If the two layers are right, the result is exact.

P(revenue > $3,000), with the settings on screen–
E[Y] = α + β·E[X]–

How to read it

Top: the pdf of X, layer 1. Center: the level sets of the joint density; inner rings are where pairs are most likely. Right: the marginal pdf of Y, obtained by adding up the joint density across all x.

The slice: at the chosen x, the bell drawn sideways is f(y | x), layer 2. It is always a normal with the same width σ; only its center μ(x) moves along the line.

Lognormal vs. normal X: with a normal X the rings are ellipses (a bivariate normal). With a lognormal X, same mean and variance, the rings become teardrops packed near small x and stretched to the right.

Step 3 · Copulas

    
Copulas: separating the marginals from the dependence

    
Now put the two previous steps together. Choose how each variable behaves on its own (its marginal) and how the two are related (the copula). Sklar's theorem says any joint distribution splits this way: H(x, y) = C[F(x), G(y)]. Plot 2 is step 1 applied to both variables at once. Change the distributions and only plot 1 changes; change the family or the strength and all three change.

    
Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions
Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.
Two variables have a correlation of 0.3. Does that tell you whether their extreme values happen together?
No. One number cannot say where the dependence sits; two pairs with the same correlation can behave very differently in the tails.
If you record sales in thousands of dollars instead of dollars, should the relationship with temperature change?
No. Changing units, or any transformation that keeps the order, leaves the relationship intact. The copula captures exactly that.
Do big floods in two neighboring cities happen together more often than small ones?
Usually yes: a big storm hits both. That is upper-tail dependence, which the Gumbel copula models.

How to read the three plots

      
1. Original scale. The pairs as observed. Above and to the side, the distributions chosen for X and Y. Pearson is computed here and depends on those shapes.

      
Least-squares line. The straight line through plot 1, ŷ = b₀ + b₁x, with slope b₁ = r · sy / sx, where r is the Pearson correlation. It describes only the linear part of the relationship, so it fits poorly when the cloud bends or the marginals are skewed, even when the copula is strong.

      
2. Uniforms. Each variable passed through its own cdf: u = F(x), v = G(y). The histograms are always flat; all that remains is how the points pair up. This cloud is the copula.

      
3. Normal scale. The same copula under a magnifying glass: z = Φ⁻¹(u). The Gaussian gives ellipses; the other families bend the ellipse in their tails.

      
Level curves. Each ring joins points of equal estimated density, like the contour lines of a topographic map. Inner rings mark where pairs are most crowded. They are estimated from the sample, so they wobble a little from one draw to the next.

Step 4 · Measuring dependence

    
The strength control: Kendall's τ

    
Before Before you start: test your intuition · 3 questions
Think about each one first, then click it to see the answer.
Pick two days at random. With a perfect positive relationship, how often would the hotter day also have higher sales?
Always: 100% of the time.
And with no relationship at all?
Half the time, like a coin flip: 50%.
Is \(\theta=2\) in Clayton exactly as strong as \(\theta=2\) in Frank?
No. Each family's parameter has its own scale, which is why the lab uses Kendall's \(\tau\) as a common scale.

    

      

        
Every family has its own parameter on its own scale: ρ for the Gaussian, θ for Clayton, Gumbel and Frank. The same number means different things in each, so the slider uses one common measure instead, Kendall's τ, and the page converts it to each family's parameter (shown in Family parameter).

        
That keeps the total strength fixed when you switch families, so the only thing that changes in the plots is where the dependence concentrates. It also ignores the marginals, because it uses only the order of the data.

        
How to read it. Pick two observations at random, for example two days. τ compares the chance that they move in the same direction with the chance that they move in opposite directions:

        
\[ \tau=P(\text{same direction})-P(\text{opposite}),\qquad P(\text{same direction})=\frac{1+\tau}{2} \]

        
The strength labels are a common rule of thumb, not fixed thresholds; what counts as strong depends on the field.

If \(\tau=0.6\), how often do two random observations move in the same direction?
\(\frac{1+0.6}{2}=0.80\), so 80% of the time.
What Clayton parameter corresponds to \(\tau=0.5\)?
\(\theta=\frac{2\tau}{1-\tau}=\frac{1}{0.5}=2\).
Why does \(\tau\) stay the same when the marginals change, while Pearson does not?
\(\tau\) uses only the order of the data, and changing the marginals never changes the order. Pearson uses the actual values, so it does.

Ten questions to talk through

    
No formulas to memorize: each question asks you to predict what you will see for a given setting and explain why. Discuss first, press Set it up on the page to check your prediction, then open the discussion points.

    

      
1Step 3 · Copulas
Start with normal X and Y, Gaussian copula, τ = 0.5. Now switch Y to Lognormal. Before you look: what will happen to plot 2 and to the classic correlation?
Show discussion points
Plot 2 stays exactly the same and so does Kendall's τ, because changing the marginal does not change the order of the data. Plot 1 bends and the classic Pearson correlation drops, since the cloud is no longer close to a straight line. Same relationship, different numbers: that is why Pearson alone can mislead.

      
2Step 3 · Copulas
Ice-cream shop: Clayton, τ = 0.6, X = temperature (Normal), Y = sales (Lognormal). A manager says: “When it's hot, our sales are easy to predict.” Does plot 2 back her up?
Show discussion points
No, it says the opposite. Clayton is tight in the lower-left corner: cold days reliably bring low sales. In the upper-right corner the points spread out, so hot days bring higher sales that vary a lot. Cold days are the predictable ones.

      
3Step 3 · Copulas
An insurer models flood losses in two neighboring cities with a Gaussian copula, τ = 0.5. Switch to Gumbel with the same τ. Would the Gaussian model have under- or overestimated the chance that both cities suffer huge losses at once?
Show discussion points
Underestimated. With the same overall strength, Gumbel packs many more points into the upper-right corner: big losses arrive together. The Gaussian has no tail dependence, so it treats joint disasters as rarer than they really are. Same τ, very different risk.

      
4Step 3 · Copulas
Set τ = 0 and cycle through the five families. Does the family still matter when there is no dependence?
Show discussion points
For Gaussian, Clayton, Gumbel and Frank, no: they all collapse into an even cloud on the square, which is independence. The Student t is the surprise. With ρ = 0 it still shows points in all four corners, because its extremes tend to happen together even when there is no overall correlation. Zero correlation does not always mean independence.

      
5Step 3 · Copulas
Take Clayton and move τ to −0.5. Which corner of plot 2 concentrates the points now, and what everyday story could fit that pattern?
Show discussion points
The rotated Clayton concentrates the points in the upper-left corner: very low X comes reliably with very high Y. A story that fits: extremely cold days and heating bills. On the coldest days the bill is almost always very high, while on mild days the bill varies more.

      
6Step 3 · Copulas
Make X Bimodal and Y Normal with a Gaussian copula, τ = 0.5. Plot 1 shows two separate clusters. Is the relationship weaker, or is something wrong with the copula?
Show discussion points
Nothing is wrong. The two clusters come from the marginal of X, which has two peaks. Plot 2 looks exactly as it does with two normals, because the copula only sees percentiles. The clusters are about the shape of X, not about how X and Y move together.

      
7Step 1 · Transformations
With the Exponential, follow y = 0.2 and then y = 3 with the slider. Which one moves more in u for a small change in y, and why?
Show discussion points
The value near 0. The cdf is steep there because that is where most of the probability sits, so a small step in y adds a lot of probability. Out at y = 3 the cdf is almost flat: there is so little probability in the tail that moving y barely changes u.

      
8Step 1 · Transformations
Choose the Bimodal and look at the cdf between the two peaks. Why is it almost flat there, and does the uniform on the right still come out flat?
Show discussion points
Very little probability lies between the peaks, so the accumulated probability barely grows there. The few values in that gap are squeezed into a small range of u. The uniform still comes out flat, because each band still holds exactly 20% of the probability.

      
9Step 2 · Level sets
Load the board example, then raise σ from 0.5 to 1.2. What happens to the rings, and what does it mean for predicting Y from X?
Show discussion points
The rings get wider around the same line μ(x). The average relationship has not changed, but each value of X now leaves much more uncertainty about Y, so predictions are less precise. A strong slope with large noise can still be a weak relationship in practice.

      
10Step 2 · Level sets
Switch X from Lognormal to Normal with the same mean and variance. Why do the rings change from teardrops to ellipses, and which one would you trust for a variable like income?
Show discussion points
The normal is symmetric, so the rings are too. The lognormal is skewed: many small values and a long right tail, which squeezes the rings near small x and stretches them to the right. For income, the lognormal is more realistic: it is always positive and a few people earn far more than the rest.

    

  




    
Classic correlation (Pearson)
Linear correlation of the pairs exactly as observed in plot 1. It measures how close the cloud is to a straight line, so it depends on the shapes of the marginals.
\[ r=\frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\,\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2}} \]
Reading: \(r^2\) is the share of the variance of \(Y\) that a straight line in \(X\) explains.

    
Pearson on the normal scale
The same formula, applied to the normal scores of plot 3. Since those marginals are always normal, it depends only on the copula.
\[ z_{1i}=\Phi^{-1}(u_i),\quad z_{2i}=\Phi^{-1}(v_i),\quad \rho_N=\operatorname{corr}(z_1,z_2) \]
For a Gaussian copula it equals its parameter: \(\rho=\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{2}\right)\).

    
Kendall's \(\tau\)
Uses only the order of the data. For every pair of observations, it checks whether they move in the same direction (concordant) or in opposite directions (discordant).
\[ \tau=\frac{C-D}{C+D},\qquad P(\text{same direction})=\frac{1+\tau}{2} \]
It is the same on all three plots, which makes it the natural measure of the copula's strength.

    
Family parameter
The number that sets the strength inside each copula formula, obtained from \(\tau\):
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Gaussian, } t: && \rho=\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi\tau}{2}\right)\\ &\text{Clayton:} && \theta=\tfrac{2\tau}{1-\tau},\quad C(u,v)=\left(u^{-\theta}+v^{-\theta}-1\right)^{-1/\theta}\\ &\text{Gumbel:} && \theta=\tfrac{1}{1-\tau},\quad C(u,v)=e^{-\left[(-\ln u)^{\theta}+(-\ln v)^{\theta}\right]^{1/\theta}}\\ &\text{Frank:} && \tau=1-\tfrac{4}{\theta}\left[1-D_1(\theta)\right] \end{aligned} \]
For negative \(\tau\), Clayton and Gumbel are rotated: \(v\to 1-v\).

    
Least-squares line
The straight line through plot 1 that minimizes the squared vertical distances. Its slope is the Pearson correlation converted to the units of the data.
\[ \hat y=b_0+b_1x,\qquad b_1=r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x},\qquad b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x \]
With standardized variables (\(s_x=s_y=1\)) the slope is exactly \(r\).

    
Probability density function
The height of the distribution at each point. It is not a probability: probabilities are areas under it, and it can exceed 1.
\[ P(a\le Y\le b)=\int_a^b f(y)\,dy,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y)\,dy=1 \]

    
Cumulative distribution function
The probability of being at or below \(y\): the percentile of \(y\). Passing \(Y\) through its own cdf always gives a uniform.
\[ F(y)=P(Y\le y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y} f(t)\,dt,\qquad U=F(Y)\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1) \]
Closed form means \(F\) can be written with elementary functions. The normal has none, so \(\Phi\) is computed numerically.

    
Inverse cdf (quantile function)
The way back: from a percentile to a value. Feeding uniforms into it produces data with distribution \(F\), which is how simulators generate any distribution.
\[ y=F^{-1}(u),\qquad U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\ \Rightarrow\ F^{-1}(U)\sim F \]

    
This value
The cdf evaluated at the value you are following with the slider. The result is its percentile: the share of the distribution at or below it.
\[ u=F(y)=P(Y\le y) \]

    
Layer 1: how X behaves alone
X is lognormal: \(\ln X\) is normal with mean \(\lambda\) and variance \(\tau^2\). So X is always positive and skewed to the right.
\[ f_X(x\mid\lambda,\tau^2)=\frac{1}{x\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\ln x-\lambda)^2}{2\tau^2}\right] \]
The extra \(x\) in the denominator comes from the change of variables \(x=e^{z}\), the same idea as the transformations section.

    
Layer 2: how Y behaves once X is known
Given \(X=x\), Y is normal around a center that depends on x. Here the center is a line, \(\mu(x)=\alpha+\beta x\); on the board the simplest case \(\mu(x)=x\).
\[ f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y-\mu(x))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] \]
\(\beta\) sets the strength and direction of the relationship; \(\sigma\) sets how much Y scatters around the line.

    
Joint density and its level sets
Multiply the two layers. A level set is every point where the joint density equals the same number \(c\), like a contour line on a topographic map.
\[ f_{XY}(x,y)=f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x),\qquad L_c=\{(x,y): f_{XY}(x,y)=c\} \]
The marginal of Y comes from integrating the joint over x: \(f_Y(y)=\int f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx\).

    
Strength: Kendall's \(\tau\)
One common scale for every family, from −1 to 1. The page converts it to each family's own parameter, so switching families keeps the strength fixed and only changes where the dependence sits.
\[ P(\text{same direction})=\frac{1+\tau}{2} \]
\(\tau=0.5\): two random observations move the same way 75% of the time. See the table below the plots.

    
Gaussian · fathers' and sons' heights
Galton's data: taller fathers tend to have taller sons, with the same amount of scatter at every height.
What you see
An evenly tilted ellipse in plot 3. Short fathers do not predict short sons any more tightly than tall fathers predict tall sons.
Why this family
No tail dependence: extreme heights in one generation do not reliably come with extremes in the other.
Try it: X: Normal (father) · Y: Normal (son) · τ ≈ 0.3
With a negative τ the story flips: think of price and units sold, where higher prices go with fewer sales.

    
Student t · daily returns of two banks
On ordinary days the two stocks move together loosely. On crash days and rally days they move together almost one-for-one.
What you see
An ellipse with pinched points in both corners of plots 2 and 3, plus stray points far from the center.
Why this family
Both tails are dependent: joint crashes and joint booms are much more likely than a Gaussian model admits, which is why risk managers use it.
Try it: X: Student t · Y: Student t · τ ≈ 0.5
With a negative τ the story flips: think of price and units sold, where higher prices go with fewer sales.

    
Clayton · temperature and ice-cream sales
An ice-cream shop. On cold days sales are low almost every time, and they barely change from one cold day to the next. On hot days sales are usually higher, but they swing a lot: a heat wave on a weekday, a beach day, a festival in town.
What you see
Plot 2 is tight in the lower-left corner and fans out toward the upper right. In plot 3 the cloud is a teardrop with its point in the cold, low-sales corner.
Why this family
Lower-tail dependence: the lows happen together (cold day, weak sales) far more reliably than the highs.
Try it: X: Normal (temperature) · Y: Lognormal (sales) · τ ≈ 0.6
With a negative τ the story flips: think of price and units sold, where higher prices go with fewer sales.

    
Gumbel · flood losses in two neighboring cities
On normal days the insurance claims in the two cities are only loosely related. When a major storm arrives, both cities file huge claims at the same time.
What you see
Plot 2 fans out near the lower-left and tightens in the upper-right corner. In plot 3 the teardrop points toward high losses in both cities.
Why this family
Upper-tail dependence: the disasters happen together. A Gaussian model would understate the chance of a joint catastrophe.
Try it: X: Lognormal (city A) · Y: Lognormal (city B) · τ ≈ 0.5
With a negative τ the story flips: think of price and units sold, where higher prices go with fewer sales.

    
Frank · weekly ad spend and store visits
A store's advertising budget and its weekly foot traffic move together in normal weeks. The very best and very worst weeks, though, come from other causes: holidays, storms, a closed road.
What you see
A symmetric band along the diagonal of plot 2 that stays fat in both corners. In plot 3 the cloud looks like a sausage, not a sharp ellipse.
Why this family
No tail dependence, and even weaker at the extremes than the Gaussian: the relationship lives in the middle of the data.
Try it: X: Lognormal (ad spend) · Y: Normal (visits) · τ ≈ 0.4
With a negative τ the story flips: think of price and units sold, where higher prices go with fewer sales.
